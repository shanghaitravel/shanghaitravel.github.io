How to Visit the Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center as an American

Home / Travel Blog / Blog Details

If you’re an American traveler planning a trip to Shanghai, you’ve probably heard about the Bund, the skyscrapers of Pudong, and the historic alleys of the French Concession. But there’s one place that most tourists overlook, and it’s arguably the most important stop for understanding the city: the Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center (SUPEC). Located right in the heart of People’s Square, this museum is not just a collection of maps and models. It’s a time machine, a crystal ball, and a political statement all rolled into one. For an American visitor, it offers a rare, unfiltered look at how Chinese cities think about the future—and how that future is being built right now.

This guide will walk you through everything you need to know: how to get there, what to expect, what to look for, and how to make sense of what you’re seeing as someone from the United States. We’ll also touch on some hot travel trends, like the rise of “urban exploration” tourism, the fascination with Chinese mega-cities, and how SUPEC fits into the broader landscape of Shanghai’s tourism ecosystem.

Why an American Should Care About SUPEC

Let’s be honest. When most Americans think of Shanghai, they think of the skyline, the food, and maybe the shopping. But the real story of Shanghai is a story of planning. This city has grown from a medium-sized port town in the 1980s to a global financial hub with over 24 million people today. That didn’t happen by accident. It happened because of meticulous, top-down urban planning—the kind of planning that Americans rarely see in their own cities.

In the U.S., urban planning is often chaotic, driven by local politics, zoning battles, and market forces. In Shanghai, the plan is the law. The Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center is the place where that plan is put on display. It’s a museum that celebrates the power of central planning, and for an American, it can be both awe-inspiring and unsettling. You’ll see models of entire districts, detailed timelines for future development, and interactive displays that show how the city will look in 2035. It’s like walking into a city that already knows exactly what it wants to be.

This is also a hot topic in travel right now. More and more Americans are interested in “infrastructure tourism”—visiting places like airports, dams, and urban planning museums to understand how societies function. SUPEC is the crown jewel of that trend in Asia.

Getting There: A Quick Guide for American Travelers

Location and Metro Access

The Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center is located at 100 Renmin Avenue, right next to People’s Square. If you’re staying anywhere near the Bund or Nanjing Road, you can easily walk or take a short taxi ride. But the easiest way is to take the Shanghai Metro. Get off at People’s Square Station (Lines 1, 2, and 8). Exit from Gate 2 or Gate 3, and you’ll see the building directly across the street. It’s a large, futuristic building with a distinctive glass dome. You can’t miss it.

Tickets and Hours

As of 2025, tickets are around 30 RMB (about $4 USD). That’s incredibly cheap for what you get. The museum is open from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM, Tuesday through Sunday. It’s closed on Mondays, so plan accordingly. I recommend going early in the morning, around 9:30 AM, to avoid the crowds. By noon, the place can get busy with school groups and tour buses.

Payment Tips for Americans

Here’s a practical tip: China is increasingly cashless, and many places don’t accept foreign credit cards. SUPEC does accept cash, but they prefer Alipay or WeChat Pay. As an American, you might not have these apps set up. So bring enough RMB in cash to cover your ticket and any souvenirs. There’s also a small gift shop inside that sells books and models, but again, cash is your safest bet.

What You’ll See Inside: A Floor-by-Floor Breakdown

SUPEC is not a huge museum, but it’s dense. You can easily spend two to three hours here if you take your time. The building has five floors, and each one offers a different perspective on Shanghai’s past, present, and future.

The Ground Floor: The Giant Model of Shanghai

The first thing you’ll see when you walk in is the centerpiece of the entire museum: a massive, 1:500 scale model of Shanghai’s central districts. It’s the size of a basketball court, and it’s absolutely stunning. The model includes every building, every road, and every green space within the inner ring road. Lights change to simulate day and night, and there’s a timed light show every 15 minutes that highlights different parts of the city.

For an American, this is the most impressive part. In the U.S., you might see a small model of a downtown area in a city hall lobby. Here, you’re looking at a model of an entire megacity. It gives you a sense of scale that no map or photo can convey. Walk around the model and look for landmarks you recognize: the Oriental Pearl Tower, the Jin Mao Tower, the Bund. You’ll realize how small these famous buildings are compared to the vast urban fabric around them.

The Second Floor: History and Urban Evolution

This floor is dedicated to Shanghai’s history, from its origins as a fishing village to its development as a treaty port in the 19th century, through the Communist era, and up to the present day. There are old photographs, maps, and artifacts. One of the most interesting exhibits is a comparison of the same street corners in the 1930s and today. You can see how much has changed—and how much has stayed the same.

For Americans, this floor is a good reminder that Shanghai has always been a city of transformation. It was a global trading hub long before the skyscrapers went up. The exhibit also touches on the painful periods of the city’s history, including the Japanese occupation and the Cultural Revolution. It’s not a deep dive, but it’s honest enough to give you context.

The Third Floor: The Future of Shanghai

This is where the museum gets really interesting. The third floor is all about the future. There are interactive touchscreens where you can explore future subway lines, new urban districts, and green energy projects. The highlight is a massive model of the Pudong New Area, showing how it will look in 2035.

You’ll see plans for the Shanghai-Hangzhou high-speed rail extension, new satellite cities, and even a proposal for an underground logistics network. This is pure, unabashed futurism. For an American, it’s hard not to feel a little jealous. In the U.S., we struggle to build a single high-speed rail line. Here, they’re building entire cities from scratch.

Pay special attention to the Lingang New City model. This is a planned city on the coast, about 50 miles from downtown Shanghai. It’s designed to be a self-contained smart city with its own economy, housing, and green spaces. The model shows a city that looks like something out of a sci-fi movie. And it’s not just a dream—construction is already underway.

The Fourth Floor: Thematic Exhibits

The fourth floor hosts rotating exhibits. When I visited, it was focused on “Green Shanghai” and featured displays on urban forests, sponge cities (a concept where cities absorb rainwater to prevent flooding), and electric vehicle infrastructure. These exhibits are often bilingual, with English and Chinese labels.

If you’re into environmental issues, this floor is a goldmine. Shanghai is investing heavily in sustainability, and the exhibits show how the city plans to become carbon-neutral by 2060. You’ll see models of green buildings, vertical gardens, and solar-powered public transport.

The Fifth Floor: The Observation Deck

The top floor has an outdoor observation deck with views of People’s Square and the surrounding area. It’s a great place to take photos and get your bearings. From here, you can see the Shanghai Museum, the Grand Theatre, and the government buildings around the square. It’s also a good spot to reflect on what you’ve just seen. Look out at the real city, and then remember the model downstairs. The city you’re seeing is exactly what the plan intended.

How to Make the Most of Your Visit: Tips for Americans

Use the Free Audio Guide

SUPEC offers a free audio guide in English. It’s not the most exciting narration, but it provides useful context. Pick it up at the ticket counter. The audio guide covers the major exhibits and explains some of the planning concepts. Without it, you might miss the significance of what you’re looking at.

Look for the “American Angle”

One thing that’s fascinating for American visitors is the comparison with U.S. cities. As you walk through the exhibits, think about how Shanghai’s approach differs from American urban planning. For example, Shanghai uses a “green belt” system to limit urban sprawl, similar to London’s. American cities like Houston and Los Angeles have no such restrictions. You’ll also notice that Shanghai’s subway system is planned decades in advance, while American transit projects often get stuck in political gridlock.

If you’re from a city like New York or San Francisco, take note of the density. Shanghai’s population density is much higher, but the city is also much more walkable and transit-oriented. The exhibits show how the city uses zoning to create mixed-use neighborhoods where people can live, work, and shop without needing a car.

Bring a Notebook or Camera

You’re allowed to take photos in most areas of the museum, though some exhibits may have restrictions. I recommend taking photos of the models and the timelines. Later, you can compare them with what you see in the real city. For example, if you visit the Pudong skyline later in your trip, you can look at your photos of the model and see how closely the real thing matches the plan.

Don’t Expect Political Commentary

This is important for American visitors: the museum is a celebration of China’s urban planning achievements, and it does not include critical perspectives. You won’t see exhibits about displacement, gentrification, or the social costs of rapid urbanization. That’s not what this museum is for. If you’re looking for a balanced critique, you’ll need to read books or articles outside the museum. But if you want to understand how the Chinese government thinks about cities, this is the place.

Hot Travel Trends That Make SUPEC Relevant Right Now

Urban Exploration Tourism

In the last few years, there’s been a surge in “urban exploration” tourism, where travelers visit infrastructure sites, abandoned buildings, and planning museums. SUPEC fits perfectly into this trend. It’s a museum that’s not about art or history in the traditional sense—it’s about the city itself as a living, breathing entity. For travelers who are tired of the same old landmarks, SUPEC offers something different.

The Rise of Chinese Mega-Cities

Shanghai is not just a city; it’s a prototype for how China is urbanizing. As American travelers become more curious about China’s development, places like SUPEC become essential stops. You can’t understand modern China without understanding its cities, and you can’t understand its cities without understanding its planning.

The Instagram Factor

Let’s be real: the giant model on the ground floor is incredibly photogenic. It’s the kind of shot that gets a lot of likes on Instagram. The lighting, the scale, the miniature buildings—it’s a visual feast. If you’re a travel blogger or just someone who likes to document your trips, this is a must-visit spot.

What to Do After Your Visit

Walk Around People’s Square

After you leave SUPEC, take some time to explore People’s Square. It’s a huge public space with museums, theaters, and gardens. The Shanghai Museum is right across the square and is also worth visiting. There’s also a small park where locals practice tai chi and play chess.

Visit the Bund at Night

The museum will give you a new appreciation for the Bund. When you see the old colonial buildings on one side and the futuristic skyscrapers on the other, you’ll understand the narrative that SUPEC presents: Shanghai as a city that bridges the past and the future. Go at night, when the buildings are lit up, and think about the planning that made it all possible.

Explore the French Concession

If you want to see the other side of Shanghai—the low-rise, tree-lined streets that contrast with the high-density models in the museum—head to the French Concession. This area was developed under foreign control in the early 20th century, and it has a completely different urban character. It’s a good reminder that even in a planned city, history leaves its mark.

Final Thoughts for American Travelers

The Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center is not a typical tourist attraction. It’s not glamorous, it’s not interactive in a flashy way, and it doesn’t have a gift shop full of tchotchkes. But it is one of the most insightful museums in the world for understanding how cities work. For an American, it offers a glimpse into a different way of thinking about urban life—a way that is more systematic, more future-oriented, and more centralized than anything we have back home.

If you’re planning a trip to Shanghai, don’t skip it. Go early, take your time, and let yourself be amazed by the sheer ambition of it all. You’ll walk out with a new appreciation for the city, and maybe a few ideas about what your own hometown could learn from Shanghai.

And if you’re still not convinced, just remember: where else can you see a model of a city that hasn’t been built yet, standing right next to the city that has? That’s the magic of SUPEC.
