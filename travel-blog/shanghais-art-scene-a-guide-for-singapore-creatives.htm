Shanghai’s Art Scene: A Guide for Singapore Creatives

Home / Travel Blog / Blog Details

Shanghai has always been a city of contradictions—old and new, traditional and futuristic, chaotic and meticulously curated. For Singaporean creatives, the city offers an intoxicating blend of inspiration, opportunity, and cultural collision. Whether you’re a graphic designer, a painter, a filmmaker, or a digital artist, Shanghai’s art scene is not just a tourist attraction; it’s a living, breathing ecosystem that rewards those who know where to look. This guide is your passport to navigating Shanghai’s creative underbelly, from its white-cube galleries to its gritty underground spaces, and everything in between.

Why Shanghai Matters for Singapore Creatives

Let’s get one thing straight: Singapore has a thriving art scene of its own. But Shanghai is a different beast entirely. It’s bigger, louder, and more experimental. For Singaporean creatives, visiting Shanghai is like stepping into a parallel universe where the rules of curation, commerce, and community are written in a different language—literally and figuratively. The city’s art scene is deeply intertwined with its rapid urbanization, its complex history, and its role as a global financial hub. This creates a unique tension that fuels creativity in ways that are hard to replicate elsewhere.

Moreover, Shanghai is a gateway to understanding contemporary Chinese art, which is increasingly influential on the global stage. For Singapore creatives, who often work at the intersection of Eastern and Western influences, Shanghai offers a chance to see how these forces play out in a much larger, more chaotic arena. It’s not just about seeing art; it’s about understanding the cultural and economic forces that shape it.

The West Bund: Shanghai’s Museum Mile

If you only have a weekend in Shanghai, the West Bund is where you should spend most of your time. This stretch of the Huangpu River has become the city’s premier art district, home to some of the most important museums and galleries in China. Think of it as the Marina Bay Sands of the art world, but with more concrete and less shopping.

Long Museum West Bund

The Long Museum is a must-visit for any art lover. Founded by collectors Liu Yiqian and his wife Wang Wei, the museum houses an extensive collection of Chinese contemporary art, as well as significant works from the international scene. The building itself is a masterpiece of industrial architecture, with raw concrete walls and soaring ceilings that create a dramatic backdrop for the art. For Singapore creatives, the Long Museum offers a rare opportunity to see how Chinese artists have navigated the country’s turbulent modern history, from the Cultural Revolution to the present day.

Yuz Museum

Just a short walk from the Long Museum is the Yuz Museum, another institution that should be on your radar. Founded by Indonesian Chinese collector Budi Tek, the Yuz Museum is known for its ambitious exhibitions and its commitment to showcasing both established and emerging artists. The museum’s signature piece is a massive installation by French artist JR, which covers the entire facade of the building. Inside, you’ll find everything from traditional ink paintings to cutting-edge digital art. For Singapore creatives, the Yuz Museum is a reminder that art can be both deeply personal and globally relevant.

Tank Shanghai

If you’re looking for something truly unique, head to Tank Shanghai. This museum is built inside a series of former aviation fuel tanks, giving it a post-apocalyptic vibe that’s hard to find anywhere else. The museum’s exhibitions are often experimental, pushing the boundaries of what art can be. For Singapore creatives, Tank Shanghai is a masterclass in how to repurpose industrial spaces for creative purposes. It’s also a great place to see how contemporary Chinese artists are engaging with issues like technology, identity, and environmentalism.

The Bund: Where History Meets Contemporary Art

The Bund is Shanghai’s most iconic waterfront, but it’s also home to some of the city’s most interesting art spaces. While the area is known for its colonial-era buildings and luxury hotels, a closer look reveals a thriving art scene that’s deeply rooted in the city’s history.

Rockbund Art Museum

The Rockbund Art Museum is one of Shanghai’s oldest art institutions, and it’s housed in a beautifully restored 1930s building that was originally the Royal Asiatic Society. The museum’s exhibitions are always thought-provoking, often exploring the intersection of art, architecture, and urbanism. For Singapore creatives, the Rockbund Art Museum is a great place to see how Chinese artists are grappling with the legacy of colonialism and the rapid pace of modernization.

The Bund Finance Center

If you’re looking for something more contemporary, head to the Bund Finance Center. This mixed-use development includes a gallery space that hosts rotating exhibitions of both Chinese and international artists. The building itself is a striking example of modern architecture, with a facade that resembles a traditional Chinese screen. For Singapore creatives, the Bund Finance Center is a reminder that art and commerce can coexist in interesting ways.

M50: The Heart of Shanghai’s Gallery Scene

If you’re a Singapore creative who’s more interested in the commercial side of the art world, M50 is where you need to be. This former textile factory complex in the Moganshan Road area is home to dozens of galleries, studios, and art spaces. It’s the closest thing Shanghai has to Singapore’s Gillman Barracks, but on a much larger scale.

The Gallery Crawl

The best way to experience M50 is to simply wander. The complex is a maze of narrow alleys and staircases, and you never know what you’ll find around the next corner. Some galleries are sleek and white, while others are raw and industrial. The art on display ranges from traditional Chinese painting to avant-garde installations. For Singapore creatives, M50 is a great place to discover emerging artists and to get a sense of the current trends in the Chinese art market.

Studio Visits

One of the unique features of M50 is that many of the galleries also have studio spaces where artists work. If you’re lucky, you might be able to catch an artist in the middle of a project and have a conversation about their process. For Singapore creatives, these studio visits are invaluable. They offer a rare glimpse into the creative process and the daily life of an artist in Shanghai.

The French Concession: A Bohemian Haven

The French Concession is one of Shanghai’s most charming neighborhoods, with tree-lined streets, Art Deco buildings, and a relaxed vibe. It’s also home to a thriving community of artists, designers, and creatives. If you’re looking for inspiration, this is where you’ll find it.

Found 158

Found 158 is a hidden gem in the French Concession. This underground complex is home to a mix of galleries, studios, and bars. It’s a great place to spend an afternoon exploring, and it’s especially lively in the evenings when the bars come alive. For Singapore creatives, Found 158 is a reminder that art doesn’t have to be serious all the time. Sometimes, it’s just about having fun.

The Power Station of Art

The Power Station of Art is Shanghai’s first state-run contemporary art museum, and it’s housed in a former power plant. The museum’s exhibitions are always ambitious, often featuring large-scale installations and multimedia works. For Singapore creatives, the Power Station of Art is a great place to see how Chinese artists are pushing the boundaries of what art can be.

The Underground Scene: Where the Real Magic Happens

While the big museums and galleries are impressive, the real heart of Shanghai’s art scene is in its underground spaces. These are the places where artists experiment, collaborate, and take risks. For Singapore creatives, these spaces offer a chance to see art that’s raw, unfiltered, and deeply personal.

Basement 6

Basement 6 is a legendary underground art space in Shanghai. It’s located in a former bomb shelter, and it’s known for its experimental exhibitions and performances. The space is run by a collective of artists, and it’s a hub for the city’s alternative art scene. For Singapore creatives, Basement 6 is a reminder that the best art often happens in the most unexpected places.

The Shanghai Biennale

If you’re lucky enough to be in Shanghai during the Shanghai Biennale, don’t miss it. This is one of the most important contemporary art events in Asia, and it features works by artists from all over the world. The Biennale is held at the Power Station of Art, but it also includes satellite events in other venues across the city. For Singapore creatives, the Shanghai Biennale is a chance to see the latest trends in contemporary art and to connect with artists and curators from around the globe.

Practical Tips for Singapore Creatives

Getting Around

Shanghai’s metro system is extensive and easy to use. Most of the major art districts are accessible by metro, and taxis are also affordable. If you’re planning to visit multiple galleries in a day, consider getting a Shanghai Public Transportation Card, which can be used on both the metro and buses.

Language

While many people in Shanghai speak English, it’s not as widely spoken as in Singapore. It’s a good idea to learn a few basic phrases in Mandarin, like “ni hao” (hello) and “xie xie” (thank you). Most galleries and museums have English-language signage, but you may need to rely on translation apps for more detailed information.

Timing

The best time to visit Shanghai is in the spring (March to May) or autumn (September to November), when the weather is mild and the city is less crowded. If you’re planning to attend a specific event, like the Shanghai Biennale, make sure to check the dates in advance.

Networking

Shanghai’s art scene is surprisingly accessible. Many galleries and studios are open to the public, and artists are often happy to chat with visitors. If you’re a Singapore creative looking to make connections, consider attending an opening reception or a gallery talk. These events are a great way to meet people and to get a sense of the local art community.

Budget

Shanghai can be expensive, but it’s also possible to experience the art scene on a budget. Many galleries and museums offer free admission, and there are plenty of affordable restaurants and cafes in the art districts. If you’re planning to visit multiple museums, consider buying a city pass, which can save you money on admission fees.

The Food Connection: Art on a Plate

No guide to Shanghai’s art scene would be complete without mentioning the food. For Singapore creatives, the city’s culinary scene is just as inspiring as its art. From street food stalls to Michelin-starred restaurants, Shanghai offers a sensory overload that’s hard to match.

Art and Food Pairings

Many of Shanghai’s art districts are also home to excellent restaurants and cafes. In the West Bund, for example, you can find everything from traditional Shanghainese dumplings to avant-garde tasting menus. For Singapore creatives, these food experiences are a reminder that art isn’t just something you see—it’s something you taste, smell, and feel.

The Yuyuan Garden

If you’re looking for a more traditional experience, head to the Yuyuan Garden. This historic garden is located in the Old City, and it’s surrounded by a maze of narrow streets filled with food stalls and souvenir shops. For Singapore creatives, Yuyuan Garden is a great place to see how traditional Chinese culture is being reinterpreted for a modern audience.

The Digital Frontier: Shanghai’s Tech-Art Intersection

Shanghai is also a hub for digital art and technology. The city is home to a growing number of tech startups and creative agencies, and this has led to a fascinating intersection of art and technology. For Singapore creatives, this is an area that’s worth exploring.

The Shanghai Science and Technology Museum

While the Shanghai Science and Technology Museum is primarily a science museum, it also has a strong focus on digital art and interactive installations. For Singapore creatives, this is a great place to see how technology is being used to create new forms of artistic expression.

The Digital Art Center

The Digital Art Center is a relatively new addition to Shanghai’s art scene. It’s dedicated to showcasing digital art, including virtual reality, augmented reality, and artificial intelligence. For Singapore creatives, this is a glimpse into the future of art.

Final Thoughts: Why Shanghai Should Be on Every Singapore Creative’s Bucket List

Shanghai’s art scene is not just a collection of museums and galleries; it’s a living, breathing ecosystem that’s constantly evolving. For Singapore creatives, the city offers a unique opportunity to see how art is being made in one of the world’s most dynamic cities. Whether you’re looking for inspiration, networking opportunities, or just a good meal, Shanghai has something to offer.

So pack your bags, grab your sketchbook, and get ready to explore. Shanghai is waiting for you.
